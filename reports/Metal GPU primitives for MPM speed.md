# Move Newton's host sorts onto the GPU first

*Research report, dated 2026-10-10. Scope: Newton 1.5.2 `SolverImplicitMPM` on an Apple M4 Max through the metalsim Warp fork (FMA contraction, LBVH) and the Newton branch (all-core color launches, chunk graph), compared against Isaac Lab 3.0-EA's Newton 1.5.2 on an NVIDIA L4. "Without changing results" means bitwise equality with the current Metal path: same particle state, same stress, same iteration count per step.*

The largest gap between metalsim and the L4 is not GPU arithmetic. It is Warp utilities that the fork still runs on the CPU. In the two-way coupling scene, **`radix_sort_pairs`, `runlength_encode` and the GPU drains in front of them cost about 180 ms of a 224 ms MPM step**, and 92 ms of that is three sorts whose keys take only five distinct values. The fix that pays most is the one already in progress: a stable, reduce-then-scan GPU radix sort with run-length encode and integer scan. This is also the only family of sorts that is safe on Apple GPUs, which give no forward-progress guarantee between threadgroups. The repo already has such a sort in the splat renderer, measured at about 2.9 Gkeys/s on this M4 Max. Second comes the solver loop. The fork's existing indirect-execution-range replay can emulate CUDA's conditional WHILE node exactly. That removes the 0.23 ms host readback taken every 5 iterations, which is worth about 10 ms per step in the granular scene, and fusing the residual into the last color pass saves a few more. Third, porting Warp 1.16+'s own key-based NanoVDB builder to Metal removes the 11.7 ms host grid build from every sparse-grid step, and its output is byte-identical by construction. Colored Gauss-Seidel throughput is last: each color is a latency-bound dispatch over about a thousand nodes, and the only bitwise-safe levers left are memory layout, ordering within a color and fewer dispatch boundaries. All of them are worth less than the first three and need microbenchmarks before any build. Taken together, and these are estimates, the plan brings two-way coupling from 254 ms to roughly 85-125 ms per frame and granular from 89 ms to roughly 48-61 ms. Both would still trail the L4's 31-36 ms, and the remainder is per-iteration solver latency that no bitwise-preserving change fully removes.

## Host utilities, not GPU math, separate Metal from the L4

The measured frame times (2026-10-10, timing-class runs) sort the seven scenes into three regimes. The two small columns are solver-bound: Metal already beats the L4 at a = 2 (14.5 against 20.4 ms) and loses at a = 8 (52.5 against 28.6 ms), where the L4 reference averages 175 Gauss-Seidel iterations per step against 77 at a = 2 ([MPM.md](metalsim/physics/mpm/MPM.md)). The mid-size sparse and dense scenes (plate drag, granular, multi_material) run at 67-90 ms against about 36 ms. In granular, the cost is a 43 ms strain solve, an 11.7 ms host NanoVDB build and about 8 ms of host utilities. The two large coupled scenes are dominated by host work.

| Scene | Particles | Grid | Metal ms/frame | L4 eager | L4 captured | Dominant Metal cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Column a = 2 | 1.7k | dense | 14.5 | 20.4 | 20.4 | Strain solve (77 iterations mean on the L4) |
| Column a = 8 | 6.9k | dense | 52.5 | 28.6 | 28.6 | Strain solve (175 iterations mean on the L4) |
| Plate drag | 279k | dense | 67.0 | 35.7 | 35.7 | Not yet profiled by section |
| Granular | 227k | sparse | 88.7 | 35.8 | 36.0 | Strain solve 43, allocate grid 11.7, scratchpad 7.9 |
| Multi_material | 179k | sparse | 90.1 | 35.5 | 35.5 | Not yet profiled by section |
| ANYmal on sand | 486k | sparse | 107.7 | 58.9 | 24.2 | MPM step 85, MuJoCo robot 18 |
| Two-way coupling | 454k | fixed | 254.4 | 73.0 | 31.0 | Host sorts/RLE/drains ~180 of the 224 ms MPM step |

The two-way profile is decisive. Its 243 ms frame is a 224 ms MPM step plus 14 ms of MuJoCo and 3 ms of collision. Inside the MPM step, "Scratchpad" takes 123 ms and "Save warmstart fields" 35 ms, while the strain solve, the only part doing real numerics at scale, takes 20 ms ([sections_mpm_twoway_coupling_now.json](runs/mpm/profile/sections_mpm_twoway_coupling_now.json)). Host attribution assigns **92 ms per step to `radix_sort_pairs` called from `fem`'s `compress_node_indices` via `_finalize_node_indices` (3 calls), 36 ms to sorts from space rebuilds (4 calls), about 12 ms to `runlength_encode`, and 40 ms to 34 GPU drains per step**. The drains exist because the fork records these utilities as host operations ([METALSIM_CHANGES.md](upstream/warp-innate/METALSIM_CHANGES.md)).

One code fact makes the 92 ms look even worse. `_finalize_node_indices` calls `compress_node_indices(NodeCategory.COUNT, node_category)`, and `NodeCategory.COUNT` is 5 ([partition.py, local](upstream/warp-innate-mpm/warp/_src/fem/space/partition.py)). The keys are therefore integers 0-4 over every node of a fixed grid that spans the whole sand bed, and the host sorts them as full 32-bit keys. A stable sort of five-valued keys is a stable partition, which a GPU does in one counting pass.

The L4 columns need careful reading before anyone sets targets. Newton's examples graph-capture the MPM step only when `grid_type == "fixed"`. Exact sparse allocation copies voxel counts to the host, which, as Warp's docstring says, is why "exact, non-rebuildable volume allocation is not graph-capture safe" ([W118 types.py](https://github.com/NVIDIA/warp/blob/v1.18.0/warp/_src/types.py)). The dense grid reads its bounds on the host ([N152 solver_implicit_mpm.py](https://github.com/newton-physics/newton/blob/v1.5.2/newton/_src/solvers/implicit_mpm/solver_implicit_mpm.py)). Granular, multi_material and ANYmal default to sparse grids, and the column and plate scenes use dense ones. That is why their L4 "captured" and "eager" numbers coincide, with one exception. ANYmal's 58.9 → 24.2 ms drop comes from capturing the **MuJoCo robot** graph: the example captures the sand step only for a fixed grid ([example_mpm_anymal.py, local](upstream/newton-1.5.2/newton/examples/mpm/example_mpm_anymal.py)). **Only two-way coupling gains from whole-step MPM capture** (73.0 → 31.0 ms). Whole-step capture is therefore a single-scene lever, not a general one.

## A reduce-then-scan radix sort is the only safe sort on Apple GPUs

### Forward progress rules out the fastest CUDA designs

CUB's Onesweep and every chained scan with decoupled look-back assume that concurrently running threadgroups are all scheduled fairly. Apple does not promise this. An Apple engineer called a look-back prefix sum that hung on M1 reliant on "undefined behavior". The assumption that "the GPU will fairly schedule them so that both make forward progress" is undocumented because Metal targets GPUs that schedule differently, and the engineer recommended "a more traditional prefix sum algorithm" ([Apple Developer Forums 672532](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/672532)). Sorensen's testing found that "Apple and ARM exhibit failures of forward progress" ([Levien 2021](https://raphlinus.github.io/gpu/2021/11/17/prefix-sum-portable.html)). A June 2026 forum post reports that spin-based schemes stayed unreliable on current Apple GPUs, and Apple has not replied ([Apple Developer Forums 831017](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/831017)). GPUSorting's author says OneSweep "likely fails on … Apple hardware" and recommends the reduce-then-scan DeviceRadixSort when portability matters. That code is MIT-licensed and wave-size agnostic ([b0nes164/GPUSorting](https://github.com/b0nes164/GPUSorting)).

Reduce-then-scan costs about 3n memory traffic per digit pass against Onesweep's 2n, a 1.5x premium at most ([Adinets & Merrill, arXiv 2206.01784](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.01784)). The Decoupled Fallback scan recovers most of that safely. A waiting workgroup spins a bounded number of times and then recomputes the predecessor's reduction itself, reaching 36.85 G elements/s on an M1 Max against 25.75 for reduce-then-scan (143%) ([Decoupled-Fallback-Paper](https://github.com/b0nes164/Decoupled-Fallback-Paper)). It is a later optimization, not a first port: at Newton's array sizes the absolute gain is fractions of a millisecond.

### There is no drop-in library, but the repo already owns a working core

No permissively licensed, stable Metal sort-pairs is available to copy. MetalRadixSort is a stable (u64, u32) single-encoder sort, about 4.1 ms for 1M full 64-bit pairs on an M5 Max, but it is AGPL-3.0 ([MetalRadixSort](https://github.com/mnmly/MetalRadixSort)). Its parent forge-sort is also AGPL, and its fused LSD passes were found to be **unstable** ("~1% wrong on random u64 keys") because ranks came from racing `atomic_fetch_add` ([MetalRadixSort STATUS.md](https://github.com/mnmly/MetalRadixSort/blob/main/STATUS.md)). MLX ships a merge sort, not a radix sort ([mlx sort.h](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/blob/main/mlx/backend/metal/kernels/sort.h)), and MPS has no sort or scan at all ([Apple forum 105886](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/105886)).

The project does not need any of them. The splat renderer already runs a **stable 8-bit LSD radix sort in five passes, with histogram, scan and scatter stages and SIMD-ballot multisplit ranking**. On this M4 Max it sorted 18.2M keys in 6.18 ms (about 2.9 Gkeys/s) and 437k keys in 0.2 ms, bit-identically across runs ([SPLAT.md](metalsim/render/SPLAT.md)). The in-progress DeviceRadixSort-style port should treat that shader as its starting point. It should generalize key width and type, add value payloads of 4 and 8 bytes, and keep the deterministic ranking. Stability within a tile has to come from deterministic ranking (ballot multisplit or split-and-flag), never from atomics. Only the histogram may use atomics, because counts do not depend on order ([MetalRadixSort STATUS.md](https://github.com/mnmly/MetalRadixSort/blob/main/STATUS.md); [linebender sorting survey](https://linebender.org/wiki/gpu/sorting/)).

### Stability is a parity requirement, not a nicety

`compress_node_indices` writes the original array index as the sort value. A stable sort therefore lists each cell's particles in ascending index order. That order sets the quadrature-point order in `PicQuadrature` and the node numbering inside each partition category, and through them the float summation order of every assembled matrix. CUB's sort is documented as stable ([CCCL DeviceRadixSort](https://nvidia.github.io/cccl/unstable/cub/api/structcub_1_1DeviceRadixSort.html)), and so is Warp's host LSD sort with 16-bit digits ([W118 sort.cpp](https://github.com/NVIDIA/warp/blob/v1.18.0/warp/native/sort.cpp)). For a given bit range, a stable sort has exactly one valid output permutation. A correct GPU version is therefore bitwise-equal to the host path by definition, provided signed and float keys get the standard order-preserving bit transforms and the -0.0 and NaN cases are checked.

Two refinements keep that identity and matter a great deal for Newton's keys. The first is **pass skipping**. If the upsweep histogram shows that every key shares the current digit, the scatter is the identity permutation and can be skipped. That is exact, and it turns the five-category sort into a single pass. Newton's other node keys are in `[0, node_count)` but use `NULL_NODE_INDEX = 2^31 - 1` as the sentinel ([types.py, local](upstream/warp-innate-mpm/warp/_src/fem/types.py)), so their top byte is uniform only when no sentinel is present. The second is an **`end_bit` hint**. Warp's `radix_sort_pairs` already accepts `begin_bit`/`end_bit`, but `fem` does not pass them, so the device-side skip is the more general mechanism.

### Scan and run-length encode are integer, exact and cheap

The scan should use the raking reduce-then-scan layout: a fixed number of threadgroups (a small multiple of 40 cores) each reduce a contiguous chunk, one threadgroup scans the spine, and a downsweep applies the offsets. That is a fixed three-dispatch shape for any n, which suits capture ([Decoupled-Fallback-Paper](https://github.com/b0nes164/Decoupled-Fallback-Paper)). Apple's `simd_prefix_exclusive_sum` costs roughly 12 cycles of throughput ([metal-benchmarks](https://github.com/philipturner/metal-benchmarks)). Run-length encode then follows CUB: head flags, an exclusive scan of the flags, a scatter at the heads, and a second kernel that differences the start offsets. The run count is written to a device buffer, not read back. Every scan on Newton's step path is an integer scan. That covers `compress_node_indices`, the rheology solver's `batch_counts` and the builder's per-leaf counts ([N152 solve_rheology.py](https://github.com/newton-physics/newton/blob/v1.5.2/newton/_src/solvers/implicit_mpm/solve_rheology.py)). Integer addition is associative, so these results are exact. Float scans are different: a parallel float scan reassociates and will not match a sequential host scan. None sits on the MPM hot path, so they should stay on their current route until a bit-exact strategy exists (a serial GPU thread, or fixed point).

For capture, kernels should read n from a device buffer and size grids by array capacity, so a recorded graph replays correctly when the count changes. All passes go into one compute encoder with no readback, the pattern MetalRadixSort uses ([MetalRadixSort](https://github.com/mnmly/MetalRadixSort)). Removing the host round trips also removes the 34 drains per step that currently feed them.

## The fork's indirect ranges already give Metal a WHILE node

### What CUDA does and what Metal offers

Newton's graph path wraps 5 × (contact solve, all colors), then a residual evaluation and a one-thread `update_condition` kernel, in `wp.capture_while`. That is a CUDA conditional WHILE node, which re-runs its body on the device while a kernel-set flag is non-zero ([NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/dynamic-control-flow-in-cuda-graphs-with-conditional-nodes)). Metal has no such node. It does have `executeCommandsInBuffer:indirectBuffer:indirectBufferOffset:`, which takes an indirect command buffer's (ICB's) execution range from a GPU buffer, up to 16,384 commands per call ([Apple docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metal/mtlcomputecommandencoder/executecommandsinbuffer:indirectbuffer:indirectbufferoffset:.md)). Metal 4 has the GPU-address equivalent, whose stated purpose is to "calculate the span of commands Metal executes in the GPU timeline" ([Apple docs, MTL4](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metal/mtl4computecommandencoder/executecommands(buffer:indirectbuffer:).md)). ICBs cannot contain an "execute ICB" command ([MTLIndirectComputeCommand](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metal/mtlindirectcomputecommand.md)), so the loop must be unrolled at the encoder level.

The fork has already built the mechanism. Commit `6bceb39d` replays captured launch ranges through `executeCommandsInBuffer:indirectBuffer:`. A kernel earlier in the same replay can zero a range's length, which skips its launches, and MuJoCo Warp uses this today for an exact per-world early exit ([METALSIM_CHANGES.md](upstream/warp-innate/METALSIM_CHANGES.md)). The MPM version unrolls `ceil(max_iterations / 5)` chunks, 50 for a 250-iteration cap. Each chunk is wrapped in its own range and followed by the existing `update_condition` kernel. When the condition goes to zero, a tiny kernel writes `length = 0` into every later range slot, and the slots are reset at the start of each solve. Because the body, the check cadence (every 5 iterations, counting from 1) and the decision kernel are unchanged, **the stopping iteration is identical to the current chunk-graph host loop**, which the project has already shown to be bitwise-equal to Newton's conditional graph on CUDA ([newton_compat.py](metalsim/physics/mpm/newton_compat.py); [MPM.md](metalsim/physics/mpm/MPM.md)). Range skipping is preferable to zeroing indirect dispatch arguments: a skipped chunk then costs one execute call instead of about 45 empty dispatches at 3-4.5 µs each.

### Fold the residual into the color pass, with no spinning

The residual is a pure per-node function of `delta_stress`, `R_i` and `D_i`, all of which the Gauss-Seidel kernel already holds. On check iterations, a specialized variant of the last color kernel can write the per-node residual and per-threadgroup partial sums and maxima. A "last threadgroup done" counter then lets the final threadgroup combine the partials and set the condition. It increments an atomic after a device-scope fence, which Metal 3.2 provides ([Apple Developer Forums 756663](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/756663)). Nothing waits on another threadgroup, so the pattern needs no forward-progress guarantee. It removes the residual kernel, the tiled reduction and the condition kernel from each check. Node updates are untouched. The residual's float summation order may change, though, and that is the one place this step can alter results: a residual sitting exactly at the tolerance could flip the stop decision. Reproducing the current tile reduction's order inside the combining threadgroup removes that risk.

### Whole-step capture helps one scene, and persistent kernels help none safely

With GPU sorts in place and the loop GPU-driven, the fixed-grid two-way step contains no host calls and can be captured whole, as Newton does on CUDA. Each of the step's roughly 1,500 dispatches then drops from about 8 µs eager to 3-4.5 µs under a graph, worth very roughly 5-10 ms, plus whatever Python launch overhead the eager path exposes. Sparse and dense scenes cannot be captured whole, on Metal or on CUDA, without switching to Newton's rebuildable-sparse or fixed modes. Those modes change behavior: rebuildable sparse forces particle-mode warm-start ([N152 solver_implicit_mpm.py](https://github.com/newton-physics/newton/blob/v1.5.2/newton/_src/solvers/implicit_mpm/solver_implicit_mpm.py)). A grid-wide barrier inside a persistent kernel would be the only way to drop below one dispatch per color. That is exactly the spin-wait Apple calls undefined, and an orphaned spin kernel has been reported to block the GPU until reboot ([MLX issue 3830](https://upd.dev/ml-explore/mlx/issues/3830)). It belongs at the end of the plan as a guarded experiment, if anywhere.

## Porting Warp's own builder makes the per-step sparse grid exact

Warp 1.16 replaced NanoVDB's `PointsToGrid` with its own key-based builder, and Newton 1.5.2 requires `warp-lang>=1.16.0` ([W118 CHANGELOG](https://github.com/NVIDIA/warp/blob/v1.18.0/CHANGELOG.md); [N152 pyproject.toml](https://github.com/newton-physics/newton/blob/v1.5.2/pyproject.toml)). The algorithm emits one 64-bit upper-node key per point, sorts and run-length encodes them, then emits 64-bit hierarchy keys by binary search into the unique upper keys and sorts those. Leaf and lower keys come from right-shifting the sorted keys, a monotone operation that keeps them sorted, each followed by an RLE. Each tree level is then filled with one thread per node, using integer atomics for masks, bounding boxes and per-leaf counts, plus one exclusive scan for the leaf offsets ([W118 volume_builder.cu](https://github.com/NVIDIA/warp/blob/v1.18.0/warp/native/volume_builder.cu)). Node i at each level is simply the i-th unique key. The CPU builder in `volume_builder.cpp` does the same thing with `std::sort` and `std::unique` ([W118 volume_builder.cpp](https://github.com/NVIDIA/warp/blob/v1.18.0/warp/native/volume_builder.cpp)). Today every Metal volume entry point drains the GPU and runs that CPU builder, which costs 11.7-13.4 ms per granular step ([sections_mpm_granular_now.json](runs/mpm/profile/sections_mpm_granular_now.json)). A plain-sparse step runs two exact builds: the cell grid over N particles and the Q1 node grid over the 8 corners of every cell ([W118 nanogrid.py](https://github.com/NVIDIA/warp/blob/v1.18.0/warp/_src/fem/geometry/nanogrid.py)).

The port is mostly mechanical, because the per-node logic lives in about 30 shared `CUDA_CALLABLE` helpers in `volume_builder.h` ([W118 volume_builder.h](https://github.com/NVIDIA/warp/blob/v1.18.0/warp/native/volume_builder.h)). It needs four pieces of work. First, a keys-only 64-bit sort, RLE and exclusive scan from the primitive work. Second, about 19 kernels with `nanovdb::Coord`/`Map` replaced by PNanoVDB structs. Third, the 64-bit `atomicOr` on mask words, which Metal lacks ([Dawn MSL 64-bit atomics](https://dawn.googlesource.com/dawn/+/b620b2fe514a5ee2e6a2d924fd998c514019da87)), replaced by a 32-bit OR on the half-word holding the bit. That is exact, because OR is bitwise. Fourth, pooled scratch buffers in place of the per-call allocation of about 7N × 8 bytes.

**Parity is exact by construction.** The output depends only on the set of unique keys, the atomics are commutative integer operations, and the CPU builder is a ready-made oracle. One arithmetic hazard needs attention. Key emission rounds `applyInverseMapF(p)` to integer coordinates, so a point lying exactly on a voxel boundary can land in a different voxel if the GPU kernel contracts a multiply-add that the host compile did not. That kernel must reproduce the host's contraction, which means compiling it with an explicit contract setting and checking the keys exactly. The parity report shows how a single ulp flipped collider normals ([CUDA and Metal float parity](reports/CUDA%20and%20Metal%20float%20parity.md)).

Hierarchy keys carry about 36 bits plus log2 of the upper-node count, so pass skipping trims a full eight-pass sort to about five. The GSPGrid-style hash-and-histogram builds in the GPU MPM literature are faster still, but their block order follows atomic insertion order ([Gao et al. 2018](https://people.csail.mit.edu/kuiwu/GPU_MPM/GPU_MPM.pdf); [Fei et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.00699)). They are parity-safe only as a deduplication front end followed by a sort of the small unique set. That is a second-stage option, not where to start.

## Colored Gauss-Seidel is latency-bound; only layout and boundaries are free

Each strain-solve iteration costs about 190 µs. That is 8 color passes of about 20 µs each, a 25 µs contact solve, and a 0.23 ms readback every fifth iteration. In granular, each color holds 1,000-1,287 blocks over 9,114 strain nodes ([loop_breakdown_granular.json](runs/mpm/profile/loop_breakdown_granular.json)), roughly 30 threads per core on a 40-core GPU that can hold hundreds of threads per core ([metal-benchmarks](https://github.com/philipturner/metal-benchmarks)). Each pass therefore costs one thread's dependency chain plus dispatch latency, not bandwidth. Two experiments confirm it. Spreading a block's work across a SIMD group gained nothing (1.0x), and running the whole solve in one threadgroup lost (0.9x), because one core cannot carry a color ([MPM.md](metalsim/physics/mpm/MPM.md)). NVIDIA's paper says the same about CUDA: small instances are "too small to saturate our GPU for a single Gauss–Seidel color" ([Mixed MPM, Daviet 2026](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/prl/mixed_mpm/files/mixed_mpm.pdf)).

Newton on CUDA has no fused multi-color kernel either. It launches once per color with `2 × SM count` blocks of 64 threads. Its only throughput variants are an entry-major SoA layout (same per-node algorithm) and a batched solver that merges colors with mass-split Jacobi and atomics, which is a different algorithm and nondeterministic. The published Gauss-Seidel speedups in graphics (fewer or randomized colors, Jacobi with Chebyshev acceleration, mass splitting) all change the iterates ([Fratarcangeli & Pellacini 2015](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/217734); [Wang 2015](https://sa2015.siggraph.org/en/attendees/technical-papers/event/techpapers/283.html)). Nobody has published a bitwise-preserving fused colored Gauss-Seidel.

The exact freedom that remains is narrow but real. Blocks in one color share no velocity nodes, so **any permutation of blocks within a color, and any mapping of blocks to threads, gives bitwise-identical results**, as long as color order and each block's instruction sequence and contraction stay unchanged. That permits ordering blocks within each color by Morton code, so a SIMD group's gathers hit neighboring cache lines; packing each node's static data (B vectors, compliance rows, `D_i`, `R_i`, yield parameters) into one contiguous record in solve order; and grouping nodes by local-solve path, so lanes do not wait on a neighbor's long root find (the paper reports a mean of 6 root-finding steps and a maximum of 19). None of these changes arithmetic, and all can be verified with exact compares. Their payoff is unmeasured. The NVIDIA paper's Table 4 implies per-iteration upper bounds of 0.43-0.52 ms for 25k-99k strain nodes on an RTX PRO 6000 including transfers, so Metal's 190 µs is not obviously behind on a per-iteration basis. The L4's frame times (granular at about 36 ms over a similar number of iterations) do imply CUDA iterates faster here. Before building anything, two microbenchmarks are needed, because no published figure exists for either. One is the cost of an intra-encoder barrier against a dependent dispatch on M4. The other is the cost of a zero-length ICB execute. A concurrent encoder leaves synchronization of dependent dispatches to the application through explicit memory barriers ([Apple docs: MTLDispatchType.concurrent](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metal/mtldispatchtype/concurrent.md)), and Apple GPUs overlap commands only within one compute encoder ([metal-benchmarks](https://github.com/philipturner/metal-benchmarks)). If barriers are much cheaper than dispatches, encoding a whole 5-iteration chunk as one concurrent encoder with explicit barriers is the next exact gain.

## The build plan, in order

Ordering follows three rules. First, the work that removes host round trips comes before the work that shaves GPU time, because a single drain costs more than many dispatches. Second, primitives come before the consumers that need them. Third, measurement comes before speculative kernel work. The first two steps are independent and can run in parallel. Every step ships behind an environment switch (as `WP_METAL_ICB=0` does today), so the old path stays available as an A/B oracle.

| # | Work | Depends on | Bitwise verification against the current path |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Harness and microbenchmarks. Per-step hashes of `particle_q`, `particle_qd`, stress, plastic state, iteration count and residual over 60-180 frames for every scene. Microbenchmarks for barrier vs dispatch cost, zero-length ICB execute cost, and the visibility of a range written by the immediately preceding kernel. Section profiles for plate drag, multi_material and ANYmal. | Nothing | Establishes the reference hashes; the existing `tests/test_mpm.py` pattern |
| 1 | GPU `radix_sort_pairs` (stable reduce-then-scan, 8-bit digits, 4- and 8-byte keys and values, device-side n, pass skipping on uniform digits), `runlength_encode` and integer `array_scan`. Remove the host-op recording and its drains for these calls. Float scans keep their current path. | Step 0; builds on the splat sort | Exact permutation equality against the host sort on recorded real inputs from every call site, plus adversarial inputs: all-equal keys, sentinel `2^31-1` keys, five-valued keys, n = 0/1/non-multiple of tile, n below capacity. RLE and scan outputs equal. Then end-to-end hashes. |
| 2 | GPU-driven solve loop: 50 unrolled 5-iteration ranges, the existing `update_condition`, a range-zeroing kernel and a per-solve reset, all on the fork's indirect ranges. | Step 0 (visibility test); independent of step 1 | Per-step iteration counts equal and state hashes equal. Pooled scratch, 32-bit OR on mask halves, contraction-matched key emission. | Step 1 (64-bit keys-only sort, RLE, exclusive scan) | Byte-compare the grid buffer and `get_voxels()` against the CPU builder on recorded particle sets, including points placed exactly on voxel boundaries. ### The three risks that can break bitwise equality or hang the GPU

**Forward progress.** Apple documents no co-residency or fair-scheduling guarantee between threadgroups, and Sorensen's work shows that a global barrier can deadlock through starvation ([Sorensen et al., OOPSLA 2021](https://multicore.doc.ic.ac.uk/publications/gpu-oopsla-21.html)). Steps 1 through 6 contain no inter-threadgroup waiting by design: reduce-then-scan sorts, raking scans, and last-threadgroup-done reductions that never spin. Any step 7 experiment must cap every spin, set an error flag, and fall back to the per-color path for that step. It must also be stress-tested with other GPU clients active, since WindowServer shares the GPU.

**ICB range visibility.** No Apple document says when the GPU reads an indirect execution range relative to the dispatch just before it. The MuJoCo Warp early exit shows that it works on this machine, but that is evidence, not a contract. Step 2 should put an explicit barrier between the range-zeroing kernel and the next execute call. It should verify with the canary counter that zeroed ranges never run and live ranges always do, and it should re-run that test after every macOS update. If visibility ever fails, the result is extra iterations, which shows up as an iteration-count mismatch in the hashes and not as silent drift.

**Float scan and reduction order.** Integer sorts, scans and RLE are exact. Float scans are not, and they stay off the GPU path until a serial or fixed-point version exists. The residual reduction in step 3 is the one float reorder in the plan. It decides only when to stop, but a residual at the threshold could flip that decision, so the stop iteration is the gate and the reduction should reproduce the current tile order. Two related arithmetic hazards come from FMA contraction. One is the builder's world-to-index rounding in step 4. The other is any Gauss-Seidel kernel rewrite in step 6 that changes which multiply-adds fuse. Both are caught by exact compares and fixed by pinning contraction locally.

## Conclusion

The real competition with the L4 is with Warp's host fallbacks, not with CUDA's arithmetic. Once sorting, run-length encode and grid building run on the GPU, about two-thirds of two-way coupling's frame and a quarter of granular's disappear, and none of it requires touching a single float. The work is also unusually safe. Stable radix sorts have exactly one correct answer, Warp's NanoVDB builder depends only on its sorted unique keys, and the GPU-driven loop reuses Newton's own decision kernel. Correctness can therefore be proved with exact compares instead of argued from tolerances, which is the standard the parity work already depends on.

Two consequences matter for planning. First, the L4's best numbers are partly the wrong target. Only two-way coupling captures its MPM step whole, and ANYmal's 24 ms reflects a captured robot step, so per-scene goals should be set against the configuration the L4 actually ran. Second, once host work is gone, the remaining gap is per-iteration latency in a solver whose colors cannot fill the GPU on either vendor's hardware. Removing it without changing results means accepting single-digit-percent gains from layout and barriers. Closing it fully would mean changing the algorithm, and that is a decision about parity, not about engineering.
