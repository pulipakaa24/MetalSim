# Measure latency, not FLOPS, against the L4

*Research report, dated 2026-10-10. Scope: metalsim's port of Newton 1.5.2 `SolverImplicitMPM` to an Apple M4 Max (40-core GPU) through the metalsim Warp fork, compared with Isaac Lab 3.0-EA's Newton 1.5.2 / Warp 1.16 on an NVIDIA L4. "Bitwise" means equal bytes to the current Metal path: same state, same stopping iteration per step. "Rounding-level" means a change confined to float summation order or contraction, with no change to the algorithm, the coloring or the stopping rule.*

Raw hardware does not explain the remaining 1.3-2x per-frame gap, and neither headline ratio is a fair yardstick. The L4 has about 1.8x the M4 Max's FP32 peak (30.3 against roughly 16-17 TFLOPS), while the M4 Max has 1.8x its DRAM bandwidth (546 against 300 GB/s). Implicit MPM at these sizes is bound by neither: it is bound by the latency of dependent dispatches and by whatever host work is left. Three facts settle it. Metal already beats the L4 by 1.7x on the smallest scene. Metal's Gauss-Seidel iteration (about 0.14 ms in granular) is no slower than the most the L4 can be spending per iteration, given that its whole 35.8 ms frame covers a similar iteration count. And the largest remaining gaps sit in scenes whose non-solver sections or rigid-body side have not yet been moved off the host or captured. A fair comparison therefore matches configuration (capture, determinism mode, iteration count) and splits each frame into kernel-busy time and inter-dispatch gaps. FLOP or bandwidth normalization belongs only to kernels a roofline shows to be bound by that resource. The bitwise-safe speedups left are mostly structural and modest. Encoding solver chunks as concurrent encoders with explicit barriers, permuting blocks within a color, and finishing the profile-driven host-work removal in the coupled scenes are each worth single-digit milliseconds per frame. The persistent single-dispatch solve that would close the rest is bitwise-safe but can hang the GPU. For determinism at scale, the current fix (striding every atomic through one SIMD-group) must give way to the pattern the CUDA ecosystem converged on. That pattern is per-world ownership, count-scan-emit for counters, gathers over precomputed indices for sums, and sort-then-reduce only as a fallback. MuJoCo Warp measures its version at a flat 36% throughput cost from 1 to 8,192 worlds. The CUDA reference itself guarantees much less than its small-scene runs suggest. Isaac Lab promises run-to-run determinism only for rigid bodies and articulations, explicitly excludes non-rigid bodies, and on the Newton backend lists no MPM solver as deterministic.

## The gap is latency and leftover host work, not silicon

### The two headline ratios point in opposite directions

On paper the cards are mirror images. The L4 is a 72 W AD104 part with 7,424 FP32 lanes at a 2,040 MHz boost, which gives **30.3 TFLOPS, 300 GB/s of GDDR6 and a 48 MB L2** ([NVIDIA L4 datasheet](https://bxin.cisco.com/c/dam/en/us/products/collateral/servers-unified-computing/ucs-c-series-rack-servers/nvidia-l4-gpu.pdf); [fpsbench](https://fpsbench.com/compare/gpu/nvidia-l4-vs-nvidia-l40s)). The 40-core M4 Max has 5,120 ALUs and **546 GB/s of unified memory bandwidth** ([MacRumors](https://macrumors.com/2024/10/30/apple-announces-m4-max-chip)). Its FP32 peak is quoted by aggregators at 16.2-17.2 TFLOPS, and they disagree on the clock ([EatYourBytes](https://www.eatyourbytes.com/applecpu-detail/apple-m4-max-40-core-gpu/); [NanoReview](https://nanoreview.net/en/gpu-compare/apple-m4-max-gpu-40-core-vs-apple-m3-max-gpu-40-core)). The L4 therefore has about 1.8-1.9x the FLOPS, and the M4 Max about 1.8x the bandwidth: roughly 34 bytes per kFLOP against 10. Normalizing an MPM frame by FLOPS alone makes Apple look about 3.3x better than normalizing by bandwidth, so either normalization by itself can produce whatever conclusion one wants.

The memory hierarchies differ more than the peaks do. Measured on M1/M2-generation parts, an Apple GPU core has about 208 KB of registers, about 60 KB of threadgroup memory, an **8 KB L1** and a GPU L2 of somewhere between 0.5 and 3 MB, depending on whose measurement one trusts. Behind that sits a shared system-level cache ([metal-benchmarks](https://github.com/philipturner/metal-benchmarks); [Chips and Cheese, M2 Pro](https://chipsandcheese.com/p/a-brief-look-at-apples-m2-pro-igpu)). An Ada SM has 256 KB of registers and **128 KB of L1/shared memory**, in front of the L4's 48 MB L2 ([CUDA compute capabilities](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cuda-programming-guide/05-appendices/compute-capabilities.html)). Both vendors use 32-wide SIMD and issue 256 FP32 FLOP per core-cycle. Apple's per-core SIMD shuffle bandwidth is twice Ada's ([metal-benchmarks](https://github.com/philipturner/metal-benchmarks)). On M3/M4, Apple's "Dynamic Caching" turns the register file into a cache, allocated over the shader's lifetime, and adjusts occupancy at runtime ([Apple Tech Talk 111375](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/tech-talks/111375/)). No independent M4 cache or clock measurement exists, so every Apple per-core figure above is a previous-generation proxy.

### Metal's frame times contradict a hardware explanation

The measured frames after the GPU-primitive work (2 s timing-class runs, ms/frame, Metal eager or captured against the L4's best configuration) are below ([MPM.md](/Users/aditya/robosim/metalsim/physics/mpm/MPM.md)).

| Scene | Metal | L4 best | Metal / L4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Column a = 2 (1.7k particles, dense) | 11.7 | 20.4 | 0.57 (Metal faster) |
| ANYmal on sand (486k, sparse) | 32.2 captured | 24.2 | 1.33 |
| Plate drag (279k, dense) | 49.6 | 35.7 | 1.39 |
| Column a = 8 (6.9k, dense) | 43.2 | 28.6 | 1.51 |
| Granular (227k, sparse) | 53.8 | 35.8 | 1.50 |
| Multi_material (179k, sparse) | 63.2 | 35.5 | 1.78 |
| Two-way coupling (454k, fixed) | 61 captured | 31.0 | 1.97 |

A scene that runs 1.7x faster on Metal rules out a uniform hardware deficit. The ratios also do not grow with particle count, which they would if bandwidth or FLOPS were binding. The column pair is especially telling. Going from a = 2 to a = 8 (4x the particles, mean iterations 77 → 175 on the L4), the L4 adds 8.2 ms per frame and Metal adds 31.5 ms. On grids this small, each color pass is a few dozen threads per core, so the added cost is almost pure per-iteration latency. That points at the cost of a dependent dispatch, not at arithmetic. The comparison is confounded by particle count and substep structure, so treat it as a direction, not a measurement.

Granular gives a tighter bound. Metal's GPU-side loop breakdown shows **8 color passes at 17.5-18.0 µs each, a 13 µs contact solve, and a 19 µs residual** every fifth iteration, over 9,114 strain nodes in 1,000-1,287 blocks per color ([loop_breakdown_granular_now.json](/Users/aditya/robosim/runs/mpm/profile/loop_breakdown_granular_now.json)). At the profile's 230-iteration mean, that is about **0.142 ms per iteration and about 33 ms of solve** inside a 53.8 ms frame. The L4 recorded 247.75 mean iterations on the same scene with its host-run loop ([MPM.md](/Users/aditya/robosim/metalsim/physics/mpm/MPM.md)). Its whole 35.8 ms frame therefore spends at most 0.145-0.156 ms per iteration, transfers and grid building included. Metal's solver iteration is thus not demonstrably slower than the L4's. The roughly 20 ms of non-solver work per Metal frame (grid allocation, binning, scratchpad, collider rasterization) is the more likely home of the granular gap. Only an Nsight Systems trace of the L4 run can split the L4 side the same way. That trace is the single most valuable missing measurement.

### What the hardware does explain

Hardware does explain the residual per-pass latency, through three mechanisms. First, dispatch cost: metalsim measured **3-4.5 µs per dependent launch under graph replay and 7.8-8.9 µs eager** on the M4 Max ([MPM.md](/Users/aditya/robosim/metalsim/physics/mpm/MPM.md)). CUDA graphs on Ampere and later launch straight-line graphs in about 2.5 µs plus about 1 ns per node, with device-side inter-kernel savings of up to 60 ns per node ([NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/constant-time-launch-for-straight-line-cuda-graphs-and-other-performance-enhancements)). Stream-launched dependent kernels show gaps of about 1-3 µs ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/kernel-switch-latency-successive-kernels-switch-latency/309504)). No L4-specific node-to-node figure has been published. Second, Metal has no conditional graph nodes, cooperative launch, grid-wide sync or inter-threadgroup forward-progress guarantee. Sorensen's group observed Apple GPUs failing forward progress ([Levien 2021](https://raphlinus.github.io/gpu/2021/11/17/prefix-sum-portable.html)), and a June 2026 developer report found spin schemes still unreliable, with no Apple answer ([Apple Developer Forums 831017](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/831017)). Every color boundary is therefore a real dispatch on Metal. On the L4, a cooperative kernel could in principle replace it, although Newton does not do so. Third, the small L1 and L2 penalize gather-heavy kernels, BVH traversal and collider queries, whose working sets of a few to tens of MB fit in the L4's L2 but spill to the M4 Max's system cache (about 234 ns latency on M2 Pro) ([Chips and Cheese](https://chipsandcheese.com/p/a-brief-look-at-apples-m2-pro-igpu)). This is the likely reason the collider sections cost more than their arithmetic suggests. It is an inference, not a measurement.

### A fair compute-adjusted comparison has five rules

First, match configuration before comparing anything. Newton captures the MPM step whole only for fixed grids. Sparse scenes on the L4 run eager steps with a conditional-graph solve loop. ANYmal's best L4 number comes from capturing the MuJoCo robot step, not the sand. Comparing a captured Metal step with an eager L4 step, or the reverse, measures API usage, not hardware. Metal's captured two-way (61 ms) belongs against the L4's captured 31.0 ms, and its eager 87.2 ms against the L4's eager 73.0 ms. By that matched measure, eager two-way is only 1.19x off. Second, confirm equal work. Both sides must report mean solver iterations per step, which parity already makes nearly equal, and the same determinism mode. A deterministic Metal run against a non-deterministic L4 run penalizes Metal for a guarantee the L4 is not providing. Third, decompose time. Use Metal GPU timestamps (`gpuStartTime`/`gpuEndTime` or counter sample buffers) and Nsight Systems on the L4 to split each frame into kernel-busy time per section and inter-dispatch gaps. Compare the gaps as dispatch count times per-dispatch cost. Fourth, normalize per kernel, not per frame. Put each hot kernel on a roofline. Divide DRAM-bound transfers (P2G, G2P, grid passes) by 546 and 300 GB/s, divide FLOP-bound kernels (the 3×3 SVD and stress updates) by peak FLOPS, and leave latency-bound passes (Gauss-Seidel colors, residuals, small reductions) un-normalized, reported as µs per dependent pass. Fifth, record measured clocks and power (`nvidia-smi` on the 72 W L4, `powermetrics` on the Mac) and report per-watt alongside wall-clock. Nominal boost clocks are not guaranteed under sustained load on either device. The headline should then be wall-clock per frame at matched configuration, with "per-pass latency" and "per-GB/s transfer cost" as the two explanatory columns.

## Bitwise-safe speedups remain, but each is worth milliseconds

### What has already landed

The host round trips that dominated a week ago are gone, each change bitwise against the previous Metal path. GPU radix sort, run-length encode and integer scan took two-way coupling from 242 to 76 ms per frame (a 2M-key sort fell from 9 to 0.6 ms). GPU BSR assembly removed 12 drains per step. The GPU NanoVDB builder cut granular's grid allocation from 12.4 to 2.2 ms. A GPU-decided solver stop, built on the fork's indirect execution ranges, took granular from 72.8 to 63.3 ms with identical stopping iterations. And the solver loop can now run inside an outer capture, so two-way's whole-step capture works and is bitwise equal to eager ([MPM.md](/Users/aditya/robosim/metalsim/physics/mpm/MPM.md)). What remains splits into three pools: Gauss-Seidel per-pass latency (about 33 ms of granular's frame), non-solver sections (about 20 ms of granular), and coupled-scene specifics (two-way's frame cost grows from about 53 to 62 ms over the run, and ANYmal's robot side).

### Exact launch-structure changes come first

Newton's colored Gauss-Seidel writes velocities with plain `+=` under a geometric coloring. That coloring makes writes within a color exclusive, and each thread walks its strain-node range sequentially ([Newton implicit_mpm](https://github.com/newton-physics/newton/tree/main/newton/_src/solvers/implicit_mpm)). Two consequences follow. Any change to how colors are launched leaves results bitwise identical, as long as color order, per-node arithmetic and FMA contraction are unchanged. And any permutation of blocks within a color, or any mapping of blocks to threads, is equally exact.

The highest-value exact change still unbuilt is to **encode each 5-iteration chunk as a single concurrent compute encoder with `memoryBarrier(scope: .buffers)` only on true read-after-write edges**, in place of one dispatch boundary per color. A concurrent encoder leaves dependency ordering to explicit barriers ([Apple: MTLDispatchType.concurrent](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metal/mtldispatchtype/concurrent.md); [mlx-native docs](https://docs.rs/mlx-native/0.9.5/mlx_native/struct.CommandEncoder.html)). Nobody has published what an intra-encoder barrier costs on any Apple GPU, so its value is unknown. A bound helps. Each color pass costs 17.5 µs, of which 3-4.5 µs is the measured graph dispatch floor. Removing the floor entirely would cut granular's solve by about 17-25%, or **roughly 6-8 ms per frame**. A barrier that costs half a dispatch would deliver half that. A microbenchmark of barrier against dependent dispatch on the M4 Max decides whether to build it.

The second exact lever is memory locality within a color. Sorting blocks by Morton code, packing each node's static data into one contiguous record in solve order, and grouping nodes by local-solve path all change no arithmetic. The NVIDIA Mixed-MPM paper reports a mean of 6 and a maximum of 19 root-finding steps per node, so path grouping keeps lanes from idling behind a neighbor's long solve ([Mixed MPM, Daviet 2026](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/prl/mixed_mpm/files/mixed_mpm.pdf)). Newton itself ships an entry-major SoA reordering of the strain matrix that "statically unrolls the velocity gather loop for coalesced memory access", with an unquantified claim of "significant speedups on higher-order bases" ([Newton solve_rheology.py](https://github.com/newton-physics/newton/tree/main/newton/_src/solvers/implicit_mpm)). Whether that reordering preserves per-node accumulation order has not been checked. With about 30 threads per core per color, these passes are latency-bound, so locality gains are capped. Expect **0-10% of solve time**. The project's own experiments set the ceiling: spreading a block across a SIMD-group gained nothing eagerly and 10% under the graph, and running the whole solve in one threadgroup lost 10% ([MPM.md](/Users/aditya/robosim/metalsim/physics/mpm/MPM.md)).

The third exact lever is the one that would close the solver gap outright: a persistent kernel that runs all colors and iterations with a grid-wide spin barrier between colors. Exclusive writes make it **bitwise-identical**. The inter-block barrier literature reports 3-7x faster synchronization than kernel relaunch ([Xiao & Feng](https://eprints.cs.vt.edu/archive/00001087/01/TR_GPU_synchronization.pdf)). But without co-residency guarantees, spinning blocks starve unscheduled ones and deadlock ([Sorensen et al., OOPSLA 2016](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~afd/homepages/papers/pdfs/2016/OOPSLA.pdf)). Dynamic Caching makes M4 co-residency impossible to compute statically. It belongs behind a bounded-spin fallback that aborts to the per-color path, and only after the barrier microbenchmark shows that dispatch boundaries are still the floor.

### Rounding-level options, and where they are not allowed

Folding the residual into the last color kernel, via a "last threadgroup done" reduction that never spins, removes the residual and condition kernels from each check. Node updates are untouched. Only the residual's summation order changes, and that matters only if a residual lands exactly at the tolerance and flips the stop iteration. Reproducing the current tile-reduction tree inside the combining threadgroup makes it exact. At 46 checks per granular step and about 19 µs of residual plus condition dispatches each, the prize is **about 1 ms**. In P2G and G2P, warp-segmented pre-reduction and shared-memory accumulation, the GPU MPM literature's main transfer optimizations, deliver 3-5x on transfers ([Fei et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.00699); [Gao et al. 2018](https://people.csail.mit.edu/kuiwu/GPU_MPM/GPU_MPM.pdf)). They change summation order, and at the current transfer section sizes (a few ms) they would buy little. Changing FMA contraction in any kernel is rounding-level too, but the parity work has shown that one ulp can flip a collider normal. Contraction must stay pinned.

Several changes look like optimizations but are different algorithms, and they are off the table for parity: block-local Gauss-Seidel in threadgroup memory, re-coloring (randomized, cluster or a different stencil size), a different convergence-check cadence, G2P2G fusion, which also changes the time-step estimate ([Wang et al. 2020](https://yzhu.io/publication/mpmgpu2020siggraph/paper.pdf)), and mass-split Jacobi. Vivace's use of randomized ordering as a deliberate numerical choice shows that ordering in Gauss-Seidel is not neutral ([Vivace](https://publications.lib.chalmers.se/records/fulltext/245496/local_245496.pdf)).

### The coupled scenes need profiling, not more solver work

Two-way's and ANYmal's ratios (1.97x and 1.33x captured) come mostly from outside the strain solve. Before the primitive work, two-way's next-largest sections were collider rasterization and collider compliance at about 11.5 ms each. These are BVH- and mesh-query-heavy kernels, exactly the cache-sensitive class where the M4 Max's 8 KB L1 is weakest. Two-way's frame time also climbs from about 53 ms in the first second to 62 ms in the second. That growth signals a size-dependent cost, such as contact counts, active nodes or sort ranges sized by content, which a section profile at several frames would locate. ANYmal's remaining 8 ms to the L4 sits next to an 18 ms MuJoCo robot step measured before capture. A new section profile of the captured runs is the prerequisite for any estimate here.

| Remaining lever | Bitwise? | Likely worth (granular unless noted) | Gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concurrent encoder + explicit barriers per 5-iteration chunk | Yes | 0-8 ms per frame | Barrier-vs-dispatch microbenchmark |
| Morton order, packed records, path grouping within colors | Yes | 0-3 ms | Exact hash compare |
| Residual folded into last color | Exact if tile order kept; else rounding | ~1 ms | Stop iteration identical every step |
| Persistent kernel, grid spin barrier | Yes, with hang risk | Up to most of the ~5-8 ms dispatch floor | Bounded spin, fallback path |
| Non-solver sections (scratchpad, binning, collider) | Mostly yes | Up to ~10-20 ms of granular's ~20 ms | Section profile on both GPUs |
| Coupled-scene growth and collider queries | Unknown | Most of two-way's 30 ms gap | Per-frame section profile |
| Warp-segmented P2G | Rounding | Small at current sizes | Parity floor |

All figures are estimates derived from the measured section times. Together they put granular's plausible floor in the low 40s of ms against the L4's 35.8. The last 15-20% is the per-pass latency difference that Metal's missing grid sync makes structural.

## Determinism at thousands of worlds needs ownership, not serialization

### Why the current fix works and why it cannot scale

The coupled scenes diverged between identical Metal runs because float atomics and atomic counters resolved in a different order each run. On Metal, the SIMD-groups of a launch reach a shared address in varying order. On CUDA, the warps of small launches happen to issue in launch order, so the same code repeats there. The fix in the fork has three parts. When a module is deterministic, every kernel that reaches an atomic gets an ordered entry point that strides one SIMD-group over all tasks. MuJoCo Warp's broadphase pair list is sorted by (world, geom, geom). And the sand-to-body impulse sum uses chunked partial sums followed by a fixed-order combine (Warp fork `c160f7e4`, Newton fork `53a903d7`). The ordered entry point reproduces CUDA's warp-issue order, which is why small Metal runs can match the L4 byte for byte. It also puts every atomic-bearing kernel on 32 of the M4 Max's 5,120 lanes, about 0.6% of the machine. At 4,096 worlds, any kernel with per-world atomics becomes a serial loop over every world's contributions.

The controlled Metal probe shows the problem is not confined to large launches. With device `atomic_float` add on the M4 Max, **1,024 threads into one address gave 14 distinct sums in 30 runs, and 1M threads into one address gave 30 in 30**. Only extremely low contention (4-64 contributions per address, separated by more than 65,000 thread indices) looked stable, and that stability is scheduling luck ([research note, local probe](/Users/aditya/robosim/research_notes/Metal%20MPM%20speed%20and%20scalable%20determinism/scalable_determinism.md)). Per-world body forces and per-dof accumulators sit squarely in the varying regime.

### The CUDA ecosystem converged on four patterns

NVIDIA's own general-purpose answer is Warp's deterministic mode, added in **Warp 1.15.0 (2026-07-07)** ([Warp CHANGELOG](https://github.com/NVIDIA/warp/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). It rewrites float atomics whose return value is unused into scatter records keyed by (destination, thread id), followed by a CUB radix sort and a reduce-by-key. Counters become a two-pass count, scan and re-execute ([Warp design doc](https://github.com/NVIDIA/warp/blob/main/design/deterministic-execution.md)). Its cost profile cuts both ways. On an RTX 4090 it is faster than native atomics under heavy contention (0.19 against 1.46 ms for 1M writes into one address) and **10-24x slower at low contention** (0.2 against 0.02 ms for 1M writes into 65,536 addresses). Counters cost 6-8x ([Warp docs](https://github.com/NVIDIA/warp/blob/main/docs/user_guide/execution_and_performance/deterministic_execution.rst)). Warp considered and rejected serialized atomics as "prohibitively slow on GPU", the same verdict the ordered-launch fix faces at scale. It rejected fixed point as losing "precision/range" ([Warp design doc](https://github.com/NVIDIA/warp/blob/main/design/deterministic-execution.md)).

Applied blindly to a full physics pipeline, the global rewrite fails. In MuJoCo Warp it broke codegen on mixed max/add sensor kernels and silently produced zero contacts in the convex narrowphase. A maintainer said MuJoCo Warp "currently does not support warp's determinism functionality" ([mujoco_warp #1635](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_warp/issues/1635); [#562](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_warp/issues/562)). The design that works is hand-built. It sorts contacts into a stable permutation after the narrowphase, replaces atomic row allocation with count-scan-emit, and replaces the solver's float accumulations with **gathers over a per-step column index, summed in ascending source order** ([PR #1422](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_warp/pull/1422)). The second iteration of that design costs **−14% at 1 world, −34% at 256, and −36% at 1,024 and 8,192 worlds** on an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell, "roughly flat in world count", down from −81% at 8,192 in the first version ([PR #1425](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_warp/pull/1425)). With the follow-up PR, 1-, 9- and 144-world runs are bit-identical. That is batch-size invariance, a stronger property than run-to-run ([#562 comment](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_warp/issues/562)). The same pattern appears elsewhere. CUB's reductions use fixed hierarchical trees instead of atomics, at slight cost. Its cross-GPU mode adds 20-30% with binned accumulators ([NVIDIA CCCL blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/controlling-floating-point-determinism-in-nvidia-cccl/)). And molecular dynamics codes use 64-bit fixed-point atomics (34 fractional bits in Tinker-HP), because integer addition is associative ([Tinker-HP](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.01207); [AMBER](https://ambermd.org/GPULogistics.php)).

### The Metal design: per-world ownership first, fallbacks by kernel type

The four patterns map onto Metal with one hardware constraint. A local compile probe on the M4 Max (Apple9, MSL 3.1-4.0) found **no 64-bit atomic add, no 64-bit CAS and no threadgroup float atomics**. Only non-returning `atomic_ulong` min/max and device `atomic_float` add exist ([research note, local probe](/Users/aditya/robosim/research_notes/Metal%20MPM%20speed%20and%20scalable%20determinism/scalable_determinism.md); [Dawn MSL 64-bit atomics](https://dawn.googlesource.com/dawn.git/+/b620b2fe514a5ee2e6a2d924fd998c514019da87%5E%21/)). Given that constraint, the design is a ladder, applied per kernel and cheapest first.

The first rung is **per-world ordered ownership**. Most atomics in a batched robot step target per-world addresses: body forces, dof accumulators, per-world contact counters. Launching the ordered entry point with one SIMD-group per world, instead of one for the whole launch, keeps the within-world order identical to today's (task order, lane order within each stride). It runs worlds in parallel, and it makes each world's result independent of how many neighbors it has, which gives batch-size invariance for free. Its cost is the serial depth of the busiest world, not the sum over worlds. At thousands of worlds it fills the GPU. For the ANYmal-scale coupled scene (one world, about 45 broadphase pairs, 36 dofs) it equals the current fix. This is the smallest change from the existing code, and it keeps single-world bitwise agreement with the L4's warp-issue order.

The second rung is **count-scan-emit** for global append counters (contact slots, broadphase pairs). Count per world, run an exclusive scan with the fork's existing reduce-then-scan integer scan, then emit. The result is world-major and order-fixed. The scan is exact, and it runs at the sort/scan throughput already measured (2M keys in 0.6 ms).

The third rung is **gathers over a precomputed CSR or transpose index** for float sums with many contributors per destination. MuJoCo Warp's constraint accumulations and MPM's grid-to-body impulse belong here, and the latter is already done for two-way as chunked partials plus a fixed combine. The −36% flat cost on CUDA is the best available estimate. It should be cheaper on Metal relative to the atomic baseline, because Metal's atomic float path has no fast hardware reduction to lose.

The fourth rung is **sort-then-segmented-reduce**, for irregular scatters where no static index exists. It reuses the fork's stable radix sort. Expect per-launch costs on the order of a few tenths of a millisecond, similar to Warp's 0.08-0.2 ms on a 4090. That is too much to apply to every kernel, which is why it is a fallback.

Alongside the ladder, **64-bit fixed point synthesized from 32-bit atomics** is a cheap option for scalar force accumulation, and it is an inference not tested here. Each contribution is converted once to a 64-bit integer. The low word is atomically added, a carry is added to the high word when the returned old value wraps, and the contribution's own high part is added to the high word. Integer addition commutes, so the final pair is exact in any order, provided nothing reads it before the kernel ends. Turner measured a 32-bit workaround of this kind at roughly 2.5x the bandwidth and 5x the latency of a native atomic ([metal-benchmarks](https://github.com/philipturner/metal-benchmarks); [AtomicsWorkaround](https://github.com/philipturner/ue5-nanite-macos/blob/main/AtomicsWorkaround)). That is still far cheaper than serialization. Converting to fixed point is a rounding-level change relative to the float sum, so it suits quantities where parity is judged statistically, not bitwise. Finally, replace `simd_sum` with an explicit `simd_shuffle_xor` butterfly in deterministic paths. Apple documents no combining order for `simd_sum`, and shuffle behavior already differs between M1/M2 and M4 ([pytorch #150566](https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch/pull/150566)).

Two implementation hazards from the CUDA side apply directly. Determinism settings must take effect before model-constant precomputation, or per-world constants already differ ([Newton #4631](https://github.com/newton-physics/newton/issues/4631)). And module caches must be keyed on the determinism mode, because MuJoCo Warp hit silent reuse of non-deterministic binaries ([#1590](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_warp/issues/1590)). The fork's hash salt for deterministic modules already covers the second.

### What the CUDA reference actually guarantees at scale

Less than the L4's byte-identical small runs imply. Isaac Lab 3.0 guarantees identical results on the same hardware and Isaac Sim version **only for "scenes with rigid bodies and articulations"**. It states that PhysX "does not guarantee determinism for any scene with non-rigid bodies", that enhanced determinism is "best-effort ... and not verified end to end", and that on the Newton backend determinism is supported for MJWarp (with sensors disabled), XPBD and Featherstone, with other solvers raising an error. Its `--deterministic` flag simply sets Warp's `RUN_TO_RUN` mode ([Isaac Lab 3.0 reproducibility](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/release/3.0.0/source/features/reproducibility.html)). `SolverImplicitMPM` is not on that list. Newton's own developer says only that MPM "should be deterministic by default (at least it used to, I have not run determinism tests recently)" ([Newton #3561](https://github.com/newton-physics/newton/issues/3561)).

The rigid side is shakier still. MuJoCo Warp's released docs say plainly that GPU results are not deterministic and recommend the CPU device ([MuJoCo Warp docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/mjwarp/index.html)). On an RTX PRO 6000, nine identical replica worlds diverge at the first substep without the unmerged determinism PRs, and batch size changes a world's trajectory ([#562 comment](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_warp/issues/562)). Isaac Lab's "supported" claim therefore rests on Warp's global mode, which MuJoCo Warp's maintainers say their code does not yet support. The L4's byte-identical repeats of ANYmal and the MPM scenes are real but empirical: one world and small launches, where warps happen to issue in order. Nothing in the stack promises they persist at 4,096 worlds.

The practical conclusion is that bitwise parity with the L4 is a small-scene property, and at robot-learning scale parity is necessarily statistical. metalsim is free to choose any fixed accumulation order for batched runs, such as per-world ownership, gathers or fixed point, without losing parity. Bitwise agreement with CUDA's warp-issue order matters only for the single-world validation scenes where the ordered entry point already provides it. Isaac's own `RUN_TO_RUN` mode, where it works, is a sort-based order no Metal design would reproduce anyway.

## Conclusion

The remaining question about the L4 has changed from "is Apple's GPU slower?" to "where does each side spend time it does not need?" Matched-configuration, per-pass accounting shows the M4 Max competitive per solver iteration and ahead on the smallest scene. The residual gap concentrates in non-solver sections, coupled-scene growth and the one structural deficit Metal cannot engineer around safely: no grid-wide sync, so every color is a dispatch. That makes the L4 Nsight trace and the M4 barrier microbenchmark the two measurements that decide the next month of work. More kernel tuning before them is guesswork.

On determinism, the reference stack's guarantees end exactly where robot learning begins, at thousands of worlds with non-rigid bodies. metalsim can therefore offer something Isaac Lab does not: run-to-run and batch-size-invariant coupled rigid+MPM simulation. Per-world ownership plus gathers can deliver it, at a cost the CUDA precedent puts near a flat third of throughput, and probably less where the per-world serial depth is short. That is a feature to measure and advertise, not merely a parity gap to close.
